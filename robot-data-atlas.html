How robots learn · 1961 → 2030

One robot hand practised for 13,000 simulated years.

The largest real-robot dataset reported in 2025 holds about 31 years of experience. This atlas shows how the data that trains robots changed: where it comes from, how big it is, what it records, and how much a robot still needs before it works somewhere new.

The data river

Where a robot’s learning comes from, 2012 → 2030

A decade ago, most of a robot’s competence was written by engineers. Simulation took over dexterity and locomotion, then real teleoperation took over manipulation, and now human video and generated worlds are widening the river. Move across it to read the mix.

The shape of a training set

From a pillar to a pyramid

The layers of a training set show where the cheap, broad data sits and where the scarce, precise data sits. NVIDIA’s GR00T N1 paper calls the 2025 version a “data pyramid”: web and human video at the base, synthetic trajectories in the middle, real teleoperation at the peak.

Width is relative volume, log-compressed, and editorial. The 2030 shape is a projection.

Simulation fidelity

Then and now, side by side

Drag the divider. On the left, a 2012-style simulation: one environment, rigid boxes, flat shading. On the right, the 2026 toolkit: thousands of parallel worlds, cloth, fluids and lighting. One axis has barely moved: contact, the physics of touching things.

Editorial scores (0–5) based on the milestones cited. The contact gap is documented: a 2026 world-simulator roadmap notes that existing simulators “still struggle with contact dynamics, deformable objects, fine-grained visual appearance, and even the robot’s own actuation.”

Anatomy of a data point

What one sample records, then and now

The type of real data has changed as much as its size. It started as one grainy camera and a steering label. Today a sample can hold three synchronized video streams, language, joint angles and tactile images. Or the robot is absent altogether, and a person wearing a headset provides the demonstration.

Impact on real-world deployment

Bigger pretraining, shorter last mile

What matters on a factory floor is how much new data a robot needs before it does your task. In 2016 every skill needed its own data farm. By 2026 a model pretrained on hundreds of thousands of hours is reported to adapt to a new robot in about an hour.

Scale. Top bulb is log-scaled pretraining hours. The bottom bulb is relative and editorial because early systems count grasps and episodes, not hours.

Scaling behaviour

More data only helps a big enough brain

Generalist reported that GEN-0’s downstream error falls on a predictable power law as pretraining data grows. Below about 7 billion parameters, the models “ossify” and stop absorbing new data. Slide the data volume and compare.

Diversity vs volume

564 rooms beat 20 rooms

Each square is one real scene in DROID: a kitchen, office, bedroom or lab across 52 buildings. The authors trained on a 20-scene slice (7,362 trajectories) and on the full 564 scenes, and found that scene diversity improves robustness. RT-1 reached a similar result earlier: removing tasks hurt more than halving the data.
